What this is

An instrument, not an opinion

Goalpost points at any LLM-based screening system its operator controls and measures one property: repeat-stability. Run the same CV through it five times — do the decision, the reasons, and the advice (what the candidate should change) come back the same?

Why that matters: the right to contest an automated decision assumes the decision and its explanation hold still long enough to be contested. Advice that reshuffles on every run isn't guidance — and regulation that demands "reasons and a route to challenge" almost never asks whether either survives the same case being run twice.

It grew out of a 2026 undergraduate dissertation that found reasons far more stable than advice (0.89 vs 0.36). Goalpost generalises that one lab result into a tool anyone can point at a real system — for about the price of a coffee per audit.

The integrity mechanism

The gate: measurements that can refuse

The obvious attack on this method: maybe your reader is the unstable one. A sloppy reader would read the same reply twice and produce two different lists — manufacturing "instability" out of nothing. So before any claim is certified, the reader must agree with itself at ≥ 0.90 on re-reads of the very same responses — and to certify a claim of instability, it must clear a stricter margin still. Below either threshold, the instrument prints WITHHELD instead of a number. The bar was fixed before the first audit and has never moved.

27 Jul
No #1 — the strictest rule fired on the favourite finding.

The reader passed the 0.90 bar (0.904) but fell 0.051 short of the stricter margin required to certify a claim of instability — so the gate refused the project's own headline finding specifically. We rebuilt the reader and passed — but the rebuild studied the target's transcripts to do it. A reader tailored to a target reads that target suspiciously well. The author caught this selection effect in draft review.

08 Aug
No #2 — the selection effect, proven.

Point the same rebuilt reader at prose without the target's structure and its self-agreement collapses below the bar. The tailoring was real. The gate caught the consequence automatically.

08 Aug
No #3 — and this time, no rescue allowed.

Audit #2 ran under a pre-registration that froze the reader before any data existed. Its primary lens failed the gate — and rebuilding is now forbidden. The pre-declared fallback lens then passed (0.989 / 0.975) — and its numbers agree with the failed lens's to about ±0.01. Both results are published together. The refusal was a fact about that reader, not about the system being measured.

What it adds up to

Five things we now know

The verdict lottery comes with the model.

Bare models flip verdicts about as often as full pipelines built on them — at temperature zero too. If an LLM sits at a screening gate, some candidates' outcomes depend on which run they happened to get.

Pipelines manufacture consistent-looking explanations.

The 4-agent chain lifted topic-stability from 0.61 to 0.99 while leaving advice exactly as unstable as the bare model's. The architecture makes explanations more authoritative-looking, not more stable.

Explanations keep their topics and flip their meaning.

The same heading — "experience", say — counts for you on one run and against you on the next, in a third to a half of paired comparisons. The chain amplifies this (0.378 vs the bare model's 0.249).

Published hiring tools quietly stop existing.

2 of 2 audited tools pin an AI model that has since been retired by every provider. Both are unrunnable exactly as shipped, and nothing in either tool would tell a deployer.

Sometimes the system doesn't decide at all.

Audit #2's tool left 7 of 25 candidates without a clear verdict — and every verdict flip happened in that group. You can't appeal a decision that was never quite made. (Its prose was also the hardest for our primary reader to parse consistently — the gate withheld that lens entirely; the fallback lens confirmed its numbers anyway.)
